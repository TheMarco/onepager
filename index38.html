Punched cards came from a loom.

Joseph Marie Jacquard’s loom of 1804 read a chain of punched cards. Each card is one pass of the shuttle, and each hole lifts one warp thread. Charles Babbage borrowed the cards for the Analytical Engine. The line runs from there to the models I design with today.

That is still the job: decide the pattern, build the machine that applies it, then check the cloth in someone’s hands.

“We may say most aptly, that the Analytical Engine weaves algebraical patterns just as the Jacquard-loom weaves flowers and leaves.”

Ada Lovelace, Note A, 1843

Three decisions and a result.

A weaving draft has four parts. The threading says which shaft each warp thread runs through. The tie-up says which shafts each pedal lifts. The treadling says which pedal to press, pick after pick. The fourth part is the drawdown, the cloth itself, and nobody draws it directly. It falls out of the other three.

Product work runs the same way. Most of what I do is the first three parts: how a system is set up, the rules it follows, the order things happen in. Then I check the cloth with the people who will use it.
